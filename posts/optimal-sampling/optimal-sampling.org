* Preliminaries
** De Bruin graphs
*Notation.*
We consider strings (or words) over an *alphabet* $A$ of size $\sigma$.
A *\(k\)-mer* is a string of length $k$.
For $x\in A^n$, we write $x=x_0\dots x_{n-1}$, and prefix and suffix *substrings* are denoted by $x[\ell..) = x_\ell \dots
x_{n-1}$, $x[..r) = x_0\dots x_{r-1}$.
We write $d|n$ when $d$ divides $n$.
The set $[n]=\{0,\dots,n-1\}$ is the set of 0-based indices.

*De Bruijn graph.*
We start by introducing the De Bruijn graph of order $n$ [CITE], $\dbgn$.
Over an alphabet $A$, its vertices are given by the set of all strings of length
$n$, that is, $V =V(\dbgn) := A^n$. The /directed/ edges correspond to
/consecutive/ \(n\)-mers that overlap in $n-1$ characters:
$E = E(\dbgn) := \{(y[..n), y[1..)) : y\in A^{n+1}\}$.

The edges of $\dbgn$ correspond to \(n+1\)-mers, and thus are in bijection with
the vertices of $\dbg{n+1}$, and more generally $\dbg{n+1}$ is the /line graph/ of $\dbgn$.

*Conjugacy classes.*
Before proceeding, recall that two strings $x$ and $y$ are /conjugates/ if one is a rotation of the
other, that is, $y = x[i..)x[..i)$ for some $i\in [n]$.
Thus, the set of strings of length $n$ can be partitioned into /conjugacy
classes/ of strings that are each others rotation.
Each conjugacy class is also called a /necklace/ as it considers the cyclic
string obtained by "tying together" the two ends of a string.
An \(n\)-mer $x$ is *primitive* if all its rotations are distinct and its conjugacy
class has size $n$.
Otherwise, the *period* is $p>1$, which is the largest integer such that $x=y^p$ for
some $y$.

*Counting primitive words.*
If $\ell_n$ counts the number of conjugacy classes of primitive strings (i.e. necklaces) of length $n$ (for alphabet
size $\sigma$), then
$$\sigma^n = \sum_{d|n} d\ell_d.$$
To see this, there is a bijection between all strings of length $n$ on the left,
and $p=n/d$ copies of a primitive string of length $d$ on the right, offset
(rotated) by $\{0, \dots, d-1\}$ positions.
The number of (conjugacy classes of) primitive words of length $n$ now follows directly
from Möbius inversion [CITE original] [cite:@codes-and-automata prop 1.3.5]:
$$
\ell_n = \frac 1n \sum_{d|n} \mu(n/d) \sigma^d,
$$
where $\mu(p)$ is the Möbius function that is the multiplicative function
that is $-1$ for primes and $0$ for larger powers of primes. Thus, it is zero
when a square $>1$ divides $p$ and otherwise the parity of the number of prime factors.

*Counting conjugacy classes.*
The total number of conjugacy classes $Z_n$ of words of length $n$ is given by summing over all divisors of
$n$:
$$
Z_n
= \sum_{n' | n} \ell_{n'}
= \sum_{n'|n} \frac 1{n'}\sum_{d|n'} \mu(n'/d) \sigma^d 
= \frac 1n \sum_{d|n} \sigma^d \sum_{n': d|n'|n} \frac n{n'} \mu(n'/d)
= \frac 1n \sum_{d|n} \phi(d)\sigma^d,
$$
where $\phi(d)$ counts the number of integers up to $d$ that are coprime with $d$.

*Vertex partition.*
De Bruijn graphs have many nice properties.
For example, the strings of length $n$ can naturally be partitioned into
$Z_n$ *conjugacy classes* of strings that are equivalent under rotation.
This partition naturally extends to the vertices of $\dbgn$, so that we have a
vertex partition of $V_n$ into disjoint cycles.
For each $d|n$, the number of parts with $d$ vertices matches $\ell_d$.

*Edge partition.*
There is a natural bijection $V_n \equiv E_{n-1}$, where vertices
of $\dbgn$ correspond to an edges between two \(n-1\)-mers in $\dbg{n-1}$.
This way, the vertex partition of $\dbgn$ extends to a partitioning of the edges of
$\dbg{n-1}$ into $Z_n$ cycles that all have length dividing $n$ and correspond
to the projection of a cyclic string of length $n$.

*Decycling sets.*
[cite/t:@shift-register-sequences] considered the maximum number of vertex
disjoint cycles in $\dbgn$, and conjectured this to be equal to the trivial
lower bound $Z_n$.
[cite/t:@lempel71] considers the minimum size of a /decycling set/, a set of
vertices that, when removed from $\dbgn$, makes the graph acyclic. This is
trivially lower bounded by the previous quantity, and Lempel conjectures that it also
equals $Z_n$.
[cite/t:@mykkeltveit] later proved both these conjectures: one can remove one
vertex from every conjugacy class-induced cycle such that the remaining graph
becomes acyclic.

Note that even though $Z_n$ vertices can be removed to make $\dbgn$ acyclic, the
graph does still contain undirected cycles.

** The independence number
An *independent set* of a graph is a subset of vertices $S\subset V$ such that
no two elements of $S$ are adjacent, i.e. $S^2\cap E = \emptyset$.
The *independence number* $\idn(G)$ of a graph is the size of the largest
independent set[fn::Some papers use $\alpha(G)$ for the independence number of
the graph with self-loops removed, and $\alpha^*(G)$ for the independence number
where vertices with self-loops are not allowed. We use $\alpha(G)$ for the latter.].

There are a number of previous results on the independence number of De Bruijn
graphs.
[cite/t:@dbg-independence-number] determines the exact value of $\idn(\dbgn(\sigma))$
for $n=2$ and arbitrary $\sigma$,
and for $\sigma=2$ and $n\leq 7$. He proves a an upper bound for odd primes
$n$:
$$
\idn(\dbgn) \leq \frac{(n-1)(\sigma^n-\sigma)}{2n} = \frac {n-1}2 \ell_n,
$$
which corresponds to taking $(n-1)/2$ mostly alternating vertices in each of the odd length $n$ cycles.
He conjectures that this bound is tight.
He also shows that $\lim_{n\to\infty}\idn(\dbgn)/2^n  = 1/2$.
More recently, [cite/t:@dbg-independence-number-26] extended these results
to show that equality holds for the primes $\{11, 13, 17\}$ (for arbitrary $\sigma$).
Additionally, they give asymptotic bounds showing that $\idn(\dbgn(\sigma)) = \Theta(\sigma^n)$.
Independently, [cite/t:@dbg-independence-n3] studied the case $n=3$ and derive a generating function for $\alpha(\dbg3(\sigma))$.

Here, we note again that an independent set of vertices of $\dbgn$ corresponds to an
independent (non-touching) set of /edges/ of $\dbg{n-1}$.

** Independent sets, vertex covers, and max cuts
A maximum independent set $S$ never contains both ends of an edge. Thus, the
complement $S'=V-S$ contains at least one end of every edge, so that this is a
/minimum vertex cover/.

Let $V_0\cup V_1 = V(\dbg{n-1})$ be a partition of the vertices of $\dbg{n-1}$.
We can associate this with a labelling function $\lambda: V\to \{0,1\}$ mapping each
vertex to its part.
A /cut/ $C_\lambda$ is the set of edges between $V_0$ and $V_1$: $C_\lambda:=\{uv\in
E: \lambda(u)\neq \lambda(v)\}$. A /directed cut/ is the set of directed edges
from $V_1$ to $V_0$.[fn::We choose this direction to be consistent with
minimizer schemes later on.]
The /maximum (directed) cut/ is the largest (directed) cut that can be obtained,
and their sizes are denoted by $\mc(G)$ and $\mdc(G)$.
Since the De Bruijn graph is connected and every node in it has in-degree and out-degree $\sigma$, the graph is Eulerian.
This implies that for every cut, the number of directed edges from $V_0$ to $V_1$ equals
the number of directed edges from $V_1$ to $V_0$, and thus, that the directed cut is half
the size of the (undirected) cut:
$$\mdc(\dbg{n-1}) = \frac 12 \mc(\dbg{n-1}).$$

As observed by [cite/t:@maxcut-equivalence] in the more general setting of line graphs,
a directed max-cut of $\dbg{n-1}$ directly corresponds to an independent set of $\dbgn$, i.e.,
$$\mdc(\dbg{n-1})=\idn(\dbgn).$$

As we already saw, the edges of $\dbg{n-1}$ can be partitioned into $Z_n$ cycles
corresponding to the conjugacy classes of primitive words of length dividing $n$.
When $n$ is odd, all these cycles have odd length.
Thus, for any partition $\lambda$, there must be at least one uncut edge $uv$ 
with $\lambda(u)=\lambda(v)$ in each cycle.
It follows that the maximum size of a cut is
$$\mc(\dbg{n-1}) \leq \sigma^n-Z_n$$
and thus that
$$\idn(\dbgn) = \mdc(\dbg{n-1}) \leq \frac {\sigma^n - Z_n}2,$$
where equality is reached if and only if every projected $n$-cycle in
$\dbg{n-1}$ contains exactly one uncut edge.

*Conjecture.*
Since this bound matches the earlier exact results for small odd primes,
it is natural to conjecture that in fact this bound is tight for all odd
$n$, and indeed this is what we will prove.[fn::To our knowledge, this
conjecture has not been stated as such in previous work.]

Conceptually, it is interesting that when $n$ is odd, this relates $Z_n$, which
count the /number/ of cycles, to $\idn(\dbgn)$, which instead relates to (half)
the total /length/ of the cycles.

** Sampling schemes

We now have a look at the relation to /sampling schemes/ [TODO cite more?], which slightly
generalize the more commonly known /minimizer schemes/ [cite:@winnowing;@minimizers].
Given a \(k\)-mer size $k$ and a /window size/ $w$, a /window/ is a string of
length $\ell:=k+w-1$ containing $w$ \(k\)-mers. A sampling scheme then is a
function $f: A^\ell \to \{0,\dots,w-1\}$ that for each window chooses one
specific \(k\)-mer. A sampling scheme is /forward/ when adjacent windows $W$ and
$W'$ (i.e., adjacent vertices in $\dbg\ell$) satisfy $f(w') \geq f(w)-1$, so
that the sampled \(k\)-mer never jumps backwards.
The /density/ of a forward sampling scheme is the fraction of /charged/ edges $WW'$ in
$\dbg\ell$ for which $f(W')\neq f(W)-1$ [CITATIONS MARCAIS].
[cite/t:@sampling-lower-bound] have shown that
$$
\density(f) \geq \frac1{\sigma^{k+w}} \sum_{d | (k+w)} \ell_d\cdot \left \lceil \frac dw\right\rceil.
$$
The main insight is that for $n=k+w$, the edges of $\dbgn$ partition into
$\ell_d$ cycles of length $d|n$, and along each cycle, there can be at most
$w-1$ consecutive "free" edges where $f(W)$ decreases by $1$, so that the number
of charged edges on each length-\(d\) cycle is at least $\lceil d/w\rceil$.

Based on brute-force results for small parameters, they conjectured that when
$k\equiv 1\pmod w$, there exists a forward sampling scheme that achieves this
density lower bound.
The connection to independent sets should now be clear: if we take $w=2$ and $n=k+1$, then
$f: \sigma^{w+k-1}\to \{0,\dots,w-1\}$ turns $f: \sigma^n\to \{0,1\}$ and
becomes equivalent to the partition function $\lambda$.
Furthermore, minimizing the number of charged edges with $f(W')\neq f(W)-1$ is
then equivalent to maximizing the number of edges $uv$ in the directed max cut (or
independent set of edges) with $\lambda(u)=1$ and $\lambda(v)=0$:
$$
\sigma^n \cdot \density(f) + \textsf{dicut}(f) = \sigma^n 
$$

For this $w=2$ case, the conjecture assumes that $k=n-2$ is odd, i.e. that $n$
itself is odd. We then have $\lceil d/w\rceil = \lceil d/2\rceil = \frac{d+1}2$.
Given that $\sum_{d|n}d\ell_d = \sigma^n$ and $\sum_{d|n}\ell_d=Z_n$, the conjecture becomes
$$
\idn(\dbgn)
= \mdc(\dbg{n-1})
\leq \sigma^n - \sum_{d|n} \ell_d \cdot \left\lceil \frac d2\right\rceil
= \sigma^n - \sum_{d|n} \ell_d \cdot \frac{d+1}2
= \frac{\sigma^n - Z_n}2,
$$
which indeed matches the conjecture of the previous section.

The small results of [cite/t:@sampling-lower-bound] suggest that
in addition to /odd/ $k$ and $n$, the bound holds for /all/ $k$ and $n$ when $w=\sigma=2$.

In the context of sampling and minimizer schemes, $f$ is usually defined as a
"local" function taking only the window $W\in A^\ell$, without and global state.
Thus, ideally the goal is to find a "clean" or "pure" function $f$ (or $\lambda$) that achieves
this lower bound on the density.
In contrast, we typically think of an independent set as a global
solution to an optimization problem, and not every independent set will
naturally correspond to a local sampling scheme.

The Mykkeltveit embedding was introduced by [cite/t:@mykkeltveit] in order to
prove a conjecture of Lempel [cite:@lempel71] stating that the order-\(n\) De Bruijn graph $B_n$
can be made acyclic by removing $Z(n)$ vertices. In particular, it is clear that
$Z(n)$, the number of disjoint cycles is $Z(n)$ and thus that this is a lower bound.

In particular, a node $w=w_0\dots w_{n-1}$ of the De Bruijn graph is mapped to
\begin{equation*}
f(w) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} w_i \cdot \omega^i,
\end{equation*}
where $\omega=\omega_n$ is the complex \(n\)-th root of unity.
The Mykkeltveit decycling set [cite:@minimum-decycling-set] then consists of all
words with $\pi-2\pi/n \leq \arg(f(w)) < \pi$, i.e., those words that are the
first rotation having positive imaginary part.

\end{equation*}
where the $(-\omega)^0$ term is skipped.

* Comma-free codes
** Previous work on comma-free codes
We will now consider a completely different approach to solving our problem of
finding an optimal minimizer scheme, or equivalently a vertex(?) cover of the De
Bruijn graph.
Our construction closely builds on existing work to build /comma-free codes/,
which are specific /codes/ (sets of words) such that no other code words can be
found in the overlap between two code words.
Comma-free codes were first introduced by
[cite/t:@codes-without-commas] as a (wrongly) hypothesized structure of the amino acids to
avoid reading-frame issues. [cite/t:@comma-free-codes-golomb] find
comma-free codes of maximal size for odd $k$ up to 15 and show that,
in general, optimality is not possible for even $k$.
[cite/t:@comma-free-codes-eastman] is the first to show a construction for all odd
$k$. Their presentation is quite intricate.
[cite/t:@knuth-taocp4-5b-backtracking] gives a significantly simpler
presentation in terms of /dips/, which are locally maximal subsequences $w=w_1\dots w_k$ with
$w_1\geq\dots\geq w_{k-1}<w_k$. Given a cyclic word, one then finds all
odd-length dips and splits the word before each odd-length dip, and proceeds
recursively on these substrings.
[cite/t:@comma-free-codes-perrin] further improve terminology with /superdips/
(an odd-length dip followed by even-length dips) and \(n\)-(super)dips, which
are (super)dips over the alphabet of \((n-1)\)-dips using the radix order.

Meanwhile, [cite/t:@comma-free-codes-scholtz] provides a simpler technique to
create a maximal-size comma-free codes that is initially explained in terms of
"erasing commas between tokens". Again, the presentation was significantly
simplified in terms of Lazard sets
[cite:@lazard60;@codes-and-automata] by [cite/t:@comma-free-codes-perrin]. 
Their main theorem shows that the Eastman construction also arises as a Lazard set.

Lastly, a helpful algorithm is that of Melancon [cite:@melancon-thesis;@comma-free-codes-perrin;@codes-and-automata], which
gives a general bottom-up procedure similar to the ones of Knuth and Scholtz to
find the conjugate of a word that is part of the comma-free code.

** Hall Sequences, Lazard Sets, and Comma-Free Codes
Our presentation will closely follow Section 7.3 and Chapter 8 of
[cite/t:@codes-and-automata] and definitions and lemmas are taken from there
unless otherwise mentioned.
Further background can be found in [cite/t:@free-lie-algebras-reutenauer] and [cite/t:@free-lie-algebras-viennot].

*Notation.*
We write $A$ for the alphabet use $u\in  A^*$ for /words/ over $A$. A word can
be seen both as a string of characters as well as an element of the free monoid
over $A$. The empty word is written $1$, and $A^{\leq n}$ is the set of words of
length up to $n$.
The characters of a word are indexed counting from 0, and the suffix starting at
the $i$'th $0$-based character is denoted $w[i..]$.
The /radix order/ $u<_rv$ compares words $u$
and $v$ first by length $|u|<|v|$, and breaks ties lexicographically.
Two words $w$ and $w'$ are /conjugate/ when they are a rotation of each other,
i.e. there are $u$ and $v$ such that $w=uv$ and $w'=vu$.
A word is /primitive/ when it is not the power of some other word.
A /strict/ prefix (suffix) of a word is a prefix (suffix) that does not equal
the word itself.

#+begin_definition Lazard set [8.1]
A /Lazard set/ is a totally ordered subset $Z$ of $A^+$ such that for each
$n\geq 1$, the elements of length at most $n$ can be obtained via
/Lazard elimination/:
Let $Z\cap A^{\leq n} = \{x_1,\dots,x_k\}$ be totally ordered with $x_1<x_2<\dots<x_k$.
Then, starting with $X_1=A$, we have $x_i\in X_i$ where $X_{i+1} := x_i^*
(X_i\backslash x_i)$ and when $X_{k+1} \cap A^{\leq n} = \emptyset$.
#+end_definition
Lazard sets are equivalent to /Hall sets/ [cite:@codes-and-automata dfn 7.3], where the $x_i\in
X_i$ can be chosen freely
[cite:@free-lie-algebras-reutenauer,thm 4.18].

The following construction is a modern presentation by
[cite/t:@comma-free-codes-perrin 3.3;@codes-and-automata] of Scholtz' original "comma
erasing method" [cite:@comma-free-codes-scholtz].
#+begin_definition Scholtz construction [eq 7.21]
Let $(x_i)_{i\geq 1}$ and $(X_i)_{i\geq 1}$ be the Hall sequence obtained by
choosing $x_i \in X_i$ as some word of minimal /odd/ length.
Define
\begin{align*}
U&=\bigcup_{i\geq 1}X_i,
&
Y&=U\cap (A^2)^*
&
Z&=U\cap A(A^2)^*,
\end{align*}
so that $Y\cup Z = U \subseteq A^+$.
$Z = \{x_1, x_2, \dots\}$ is the set of /code words/, consisting of all
odd-length elements of $U$. $Y$ contains the even-length elements of $U$ that
never appear as an $x_i$.

The procedure can be extended to also eliminate all even-length elements [8.1.9].
Then $U$ is a Lazard set ordered via $u<v$ if $|u|$ is odd and $|v|$ is even, or if they have the
same parity and $u<_{r} v$ for the radix order.

For $u\in U$, define
\begin{align*}
\nu(u) &= \min\{i\in \mathbb N | u\in X_i\} -1\\
\delta(u) &= \sup\{i\in \mathbb N | u\in X_i\}
\end{align*}
Thus, the time that $u\in A^+$ is first "created" is denoted by $\nu(u)$, so that $u =
x_{\nu(u)}^+v \in X_{\nu(u)+1}$ for some $v\in X_{\nu(u)}$.
For odd-length $u\in Z$, $\delta(u)=i$ indicates that $u=x_i$, while for
even-length $u\in Y$, $\delta(u) = \infty$ because even-length words are never
removed from any $X_i$.[fn::Considering $U$ as a Lazard set, one only considers
finite-length strings in which case even-length elements /are/ eventually
eliminated. But here the $X_i$ contain arbitrary long sequences, and so we only
ever eliminate odd-length elements.]
#+end_definition

From here on, $x_i\in X_i$, $Y$, and $Z$ all refer to this specific
construction, and for $u,v\in U$ we write $u<v$ as ordered by the Lazard set. In
particular, for $x_i,x_j\in Z$ we have $x_i<x_j$ iff $i<j$.

We now cover some basic facts about this construction and the sets $U$, $Y$, and $Z$.

We again consider an example with this new order, showing the Lazard elimination
of strings of length at most $5$ in $U$. We start with $X_1=\{a,b\}$.
\begin{align*}

Although we won't directly use this fact here, the main point of all this theory

#+begin_definition Comma-free code
A code $X\subseteq A^m$ is a /comma free code/ when no word of $X$ overlaps
nontrivially with a product (concatenation) of two words of $X$. That is, given
$x_1,x_2\in X$ with $x_1=ab$ and $x_2=cd$ with $b\neq 1$ and $c\neq 1$, then
$bc\not\in X$.
#+end_definition
For any $x\in X$, $xx$ may contain only $x$ itself as code word of length $m$, so that $X$
contains at most one element of every conjugacy class. Thus, $|X|\leq
\ell_m(k)$, which counts the number of conjugacy classes of words of length $m$
over an alphabet of size $k$ (or equivalently, the number of primitive words
with length dividing $m$).

Let $u,v\in U$ be two words that overlap as $u=ab$ and $v=bc$ with $a,b,c\neq
1$. Then $b$ is a code word in $Z$.

#+end_lemma

#+end_proof

** Intuition

The last lemma tells us that there is, in fact, a lot more structure to our
comma-free code than just "one sample per conjugacy class": 
Any time two code words overlap, or in the graph setting, that there is a path
from sampled vertex $u$ to sampled vertex $v$ of length $<n$, this path has
length $n-|b|$, which is even. Thus, looking modulo two, $u$ and $v$ are "synchronized".
In particular, given a long string, we can consider the subset of \(n\)-mers
that is a code word. If we look at two consecutive code words (not necessarily
adjacent \(n\)-mers), they are either synchronized, or non-overlapping. 

So far, this argument does not yet give us a way to take a sample from every
context, since some strings may not contain any code word.
Instead, we can, for every \(n\)-mer in our string, mark the position/character that
starts its code word conjugate.
Now suppose we have a string that starts in a code word $c$, and consider the first
$n$ \(n\)-mers, each of which overlapping a suffix of $c$. Then, if they sample
a position within $c$ (in the first $n$ characters of the string), this position
must be even by the previous lemma.

The remaining question is: what happens in overlapping non-code word context?
In this case, the second code word is not an extension of the first and our
previous lemma doesn't apply.

** The largest contained word
With this theory in hand, we are now ready to prove that the largest code word
contained in a prefix (suffix) of a code word is a prefix (suffix) itself.

#+begin_lemma Overlapping codewords 2
Given a word $w$ with $w=z_k\dots z_1$ for decreasing $z_i\in Z$ with $z_k\geq
\dots z_1$, all code words in $w$ are contained in one of the $z_i$.
#+end_lemma
#+begin_proof
Suppose not, so $z=az_{i-1}\dots z_{j31} b$ with $a$ a non-empty suffix of $z_i$ and $b$ a
non-empty prefix of $z_j$. Then $a$ and $b$ are both in $Z$ by the previous
overlap lemma.

#+attr_html: :class large
[[file:./figs/overlap.svg]]

Furthermore, $b\leq z_j$ because $|b|\leq |z_j|$.
Let $c$ be the factor after $a$, i.e. $c=b$ if $z_{i-1}\dots z_{j+1}$ is empty and
$c=z_{i-1}$ otherwise.
It's not possible that $a\geq c$, because then $z=az_{i-1}\dots z_{j+1}b$ are two
distinct representations of a word in $U$.
It's also not possible that $a<c$, since then $ac\in Y^*$ and
$z=(ac)z_{i-2}\dots z_{j+1}b$ (or just $z=ac$ itself when $c=b$) are again two
distinct representations of a word in $U$.
Thus, we reach a contradiction.
#+end_proof


#+begin_lemma Max prefix :label prefix_lemma
The largest code word contained in a proper prefix $w$ of a word $z$ in $Z$ is a prefix
of $w$ and $z$.
#+end_lemma
#+begin_proof Prefix case
Since $w$ is a prefix of $z$, we have $w = z_k\dots z_1$.
By the previous lemma, any code word contained in $w$ is contained in one of the
$z_i$. Since $z_k\geq \dots \geq z_1$, the greatest contained code word must be
the prefix $z_k$.
#+end_proof
#+begin_lemma Max suffix :label suffix_lemma
The largest word contained in a proper suffix $w$ of a word $z$ in $Z$ is a suffix
of $w$ and $z$.
#+end_lemma
#+begin_proof
Now suppose that the largest word contained in the /suffix/ $w$ is $z'$.
Suppose that $z'$ is not already a suffix of $w$, so $w=az'b$ with $b\neq 1$.

#+attr_html: :class large
[[file:./figs/max-suffix.svg]]

Since $b$ is itself a suffix of $z$, it factorizes as $y$ or as $yx_k$ with $y\in Y^*$ and $x_k\in Z$.
If $y\neq 1$, let $e=x_ix_j\in Y$ be the first non-empty term of $y$.
Then $z'e=z'x_ix_j\in Z$ because $\nu(e)=i$, $\delta(e)=\infty$, and $\delta(z')\geq
i$ because $z'\geq x_i$ by assumption and thus $\nu(e)\leq \delta(z') < \delta(e)$,
so that $z'e\in Z$, giving a contradiction.
Thus, $b=x_k$ with $x_k \leq z'$, so that $z'x_k$ is a suffix of $w$ consisting of
two decreasing code words, whereas we know that any suffix must have a unique
representation containing at most one odd code word; again a contradiction.
#+end_proof

** Finding the largest contained code word
We now give an algorithm to find the largest code word contained in a word $w$.
We start with a one-off algorithm based on the unique factorization, and then improve
this to a streaming algorithm in the next subsection.

At the core is Melancon's algorithm, as defined by [cite/t:@melancon-thesis] and
further explained in [cite:@free-lie-algebras-reutenauer section 7.2/Ex 7.6] and
[cite:@comma-free-codes-perrin 5.4].
(Note that [cite/t:@free-lie-algebras-reutenauer] reverses both the order and
the "direction" of the Lazard elimination.)
Starting with an arbitrary odd-length word, this gives the conjugate that is a
code word. It generalizes and streamlines the algorithms of
[cite/t:@comma-free-codes-scholtz] and [cite/t:@knuth-taocp4-5b-backtracking].
#+begin_definition Melancon algorithm :label melancon
Given a primitive word $w$ of odd length $k$, start by splitting it into a sequence of characters
$(w_1,\dots, w_n)$. Then let $w_i$ be one of the minimal characters satisfying
$w_i < w_{i+1}$ via the Lazard order, taking indices modulo $n$. Then replace
the sequence by $(w_1,\dots,w_{i-1},w_iw_{i+1},w_{i+2},\dots, w_n)$, and repeat
this process until only a single word remains. If $i=n$, replace the sequence by
$(w_nw_1,\dots, w_{n-1})$.
Note that since $|w|$ is odd, there always exists an odd-length $w_i$ in the sequence.
#+end_definition

As a direct corollary, a word in $A^+$ is a code word if applying the algorithm never "wraps around".

We now slightly modify the algorithm to find code words in non-cyclic strings.
#+begin_definition Linear Melancon algorithm
Given any word $w\in A^*$, start with the sequence $(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ of
characters. Initially, all words $w_i$ are /unfixed/, and over time, a growing
suffix of words is /fixed/.
If the sequence contains no unfixed word of odd length, stop.
If the smallest unfixed word $w_i$ in the sequence is at the back or if
$w_{i+1}$ has been /fixed/, /fix/ $w_i$ itself.
Otherwise, find an occurrence of the minimal unfixed $w_i$ with $w_i<w_{i+1}$ and
contract them and repeat.
The algorithm ends with a sequence $(y_1, \dots, y_n, z_k,\dots,z_1)$, where all
$z_i\in Z$ are fixed, and all $y_i\in Y$ are unfixed.
#+end_definition

#+begin_example
For the word $011001101001001000$ we obtain:
\begin{align*}
&(0,1,1,0,0,1,1,0,1,0,0,1,0,0,1,0,0,0)\\
\textrm{merge $0$}   \quad&(01,1,0,01,1,01,0,01,0,01,0,00)\\
\textrm{merge $0$}   \quad&(01,1,001,1,01,001,001,000)\\
\textrm{merge $1$}   \quad&(01,1001,101,001,001,000)\\
\textrm{fix $000$}\quad&(01,1001,101,001001,\underline{000})\\
\textrm{merge $101$} \quad&(01,1001,101001001,\underline{000})\\
\textrm{fix $101001001$}   \quad&(01,1001,\underline{101001001},\underline{000}).\\
\end{align*}
#+end_example

We now prove that this final state is exactly the unique $yz_k\dots z_1$
factorization of $w$.
#+begin_lemma 
For any $w\in A^*$, the linear Melancon algorithm recovers the unique factorization $w=yz_k\dots
z_1$ with $y\in Y*$, $z_i\in Z$, and $z_k \geq \dots \geq z_1$.
#+end_lemma
#+begin_proof
Every word generated by the algorithm is in $U$.
The linear Melancon algorithm only fixes odd-length words from the back to the front.
Furthermore, it always considers words from small to large, and so later fixed
elements are not smaller. At the end, all odd-length words must be fixed and so
form a suffix $z_k\geq \dots \geq  z_1$, while no even-length words are fixed. The
remaining unfixed elements must all have even length, and so are all in $Y$.
Thus, $y=y_1\dots y_n\in Y^*$ and $yz_k\dots z_1$ must be the unique
factorization.
#+end_proof

#+begin_lemma Max factor at even position

If the largest code word contained in $w$ starts at an even position, then it is $z_k$.
#+end_lemma
#+begin_proof
As before, write $w = yz_k\dots z_1$.
Clearly $z_k$ starts at an even position.

Then the largest code word starts with a non-empty suffix of some $y_i\in Y$. By

starts at an odd position.
#+end_proof

We now directly obtain our result.
#+begin_theorem 
The largest code word in any word $w\in A^+$ can be found by running the linear
Melancon algorithm on both $w$ and the suffix $w'=w[1..]$ to obtain $w=yz_k\dots
z_1$ and $w'=y'z'_{k'}\dots z'_1$ and returning the larger of $z_k$ and $z'_{k'}$.
#+end_theorem


** A Sliding-window algorithm

Compared to the twice-repeated linear Melancon algorithm above, we can be
slightly more precise.

#+begin_lemma 
Let $w\in A^+$ be any word and factorize it as $w=yz_k\dots z_1$. Then the largest
code word in $w$ is either $z_k$ or a suffix $z_{k+1}$ of $y$. In the latter case,
$w'=w[1..]$ factorizes as $w'=y'z_{k+1}z_k\dots z_1$.
#+end_lemma
#+begin_proof
If the largest code word starts at an even position, it must be $z_k$, as
highlighted red below, and we are done.

#+attr_html: :class large
[[file:./figs/suffix-factorization.svg]]

Otherwise, consider $y_1 = y[1..]$ and factorize $w'=y_1 z_k\dots z_1$ as $w' =
y'z'_{k'}\dots z'_1$. Now we prepend $c$ to $w'$ to obtain $w = cy' z'_{k'}\dots z'_1$.
Since $cy'\in ZY^*\subseteq Y^*Z$, we get $cy'=y''s$ with $s\in Z$. Thus,
$w=y'' sz'_{k'}\dots z'_1$. Now, if $s\geq z'_{k'}$ this is the unique
factorization and thus must coincide with $w=yz_k\dots z_1$, giving $z'_{k'} =
z_{k-1}$, with $z_k$ (in red) as largest factor.
Otherwise, if $s<z'_{k'}$, then $sz'_{k'}\in Y^*$ and
$w=y''(sz'_{k'})z'_{k'-1}\dots z_1$ is the normal factorization and $z'_{k'-1} =
z_k$, so that $z'_{k'}$ in the factorization of $w'$ is the largest overall factor.

Thus, the factorization of $w$ has one exactly more or one less odd factor than
the factorization of $w'$, and the one with the larger number of odd factors
contains the largest code word in $w$ as largest factor.
#+end_proof

This gives rise to a linear-time streaming algorithm, where we push characters
to the right of a window $w$ and pop characters from its left.
#+begin_definition Streaming algorithm
At every point maintain a representation $w = v z_k\dots z_1$, where $v\in Y^*$
or $v\in AY^*$, such that $z_k$ is the largest contained code word.

To shrink $w$ on the left, remove the first character of $v$. If $v=1$,
consider the factorization $z_k = c_1c_2 yz$ for $c_1,c_2\in A$.
If $z<z_{k-1}$, we get $w' = v'z_{k-1}\dots z_1$ for $v'=c_2yz$.
If $z\geq z_{k-1}$, we instead get $w'=v'z z_{k-1}\dots z_1$ for $v'=c_2y$.

To grow $w$ on the right, we get $w'=v z_k\dots z_1 c$.
If $z_1\geq c$, we are done.
Otherwise, $z_1<c$ and $z_1c\in Y$, giving $c'=z_2z_1y\in Z$ (assuming $z_2$ exists).
Thus, repeatedly 'contract' $c$ up two levels at a time as long as there are two
preceding $z_i$ and $c$ is larger than the last remaining $z_i$.
We end with a new sequence $w'=vz_k\dots z_i c''$, where $z_k\dots z_i$ might be empty.
#+end_definition
